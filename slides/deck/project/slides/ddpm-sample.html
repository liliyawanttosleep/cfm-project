Sampling, and why ε is a score

Ancestral sampling: t = T, …, 1

xₜ₋₁ = (1/√αₜ) ( xₜ − βₜ/√(1 − ᾱₜ) · εθ(xₜ, t) ) + σ̃ₜ z

σ̃ₜ² = (1 − ᾱₜ₋₁)/(1 − ᾱₜ) · βₜ, z ~ N(0, I)

Stochastic, and needs T = 1000 network calls per sample.

Noise prediction = score estimation

∇ₓ log pₜ(x) ≈ − εθ(x, t) / √(1 − ᾱₜ)

E[x₀ | xₜ] = ( xₜ + (1 − ᾱₜ) ∇ log pₜ(xₜ) ) / √ᾱₜ

Tweedie’s formula: the denoiser is a posterior mean; this is denoising score matching.

Flow Matching vs. Diffusion · 7

[Kaiming · ~1.5 min] To sample, start from pure noise and walk back one step at a time: use the predicted noise to form the posterior mean, then add a little fresh noise. With T = 1000 this costs a thousand network calls per sample. Why does predicting noise work? Divide the predicted noise by minus root one-minus-alpha-bar and you get the score, the gradient of the log density. Tweedie's formula then says the posterior mean of x_0 given x_t is a simple function of the score. So the denoiser is a posterior-mean estimator — this is denoising score matching.
