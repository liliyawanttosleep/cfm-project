[Wentao · ~1.3 min] Step two: use all the pairs. Draw many noise points and many data points, pair them independently, and draw all the lines. On the left we have six lines, in the middle two hundred. If we keep adding pairs, at each time the positions of the lines form a smooth density, shown on the right. That density is p_t. The formula says the same thing: p_t is a mixture of the single-line densities, weighted by how the pairs are drawn. With sigma equal to zero, each p_t given z is just a point mass on its line. Two things make this useful. First, the endpoints are correct by construction: at t equals 0 every line is at its noise point, so we get p₀; at t equals 1 every line is at its data point, so we get the data distribution. Remember the second problem on the last slide: there are many possible paths. Now we have chosen one. Second, we do not need a formula for p_t in order to sample from it. Draw a pair and a time, and compute x_t. That is the variable xt in our training loop. So we have the path. What is its velocity field?
