Step 2. Why paths bend, and what one step does

Why they bend: the velocity is an average

uₜ(x) = ( E[x₁ │ xₜ = x] − x ) / (1 − t)

Early on, a point says little about its destination, so it aims at the mean. Later, it knows, and it turns.

One step: x₀ is independent of x₁, so at t = 0

u₀(x) = E[x₁] − x ⇒ x + u₀(x) = E[x₁]

Predicted W2 = √tr Cov = 1.41 on standardized 2D data. Measured with I-CFM: 1.28–1.37.

Flow Matching vs. Diffusion · 16 · Lines cross; marginal paths do not. W2: one step, four datasets, seed 0. The trained net only approximates u₀.

[Wentao · ~1.3 min] Back to our small example. On the left are the two lines through our point, with velocities plus 3.8 and minus 4.2. Independent pairing makes lines cross everywhere, and at a crossing the network can give only the average. In the middle is the consequence. Take a point starting at x equals 1. It will end at plus 2, but at time zero it knows nothing about its destination, so it aims at the mean, zero. It starts with velocity minus 1, moving away. As time passes, the posterior becomes sure, and the path turns. Be careful with the words: individual lines cross, but marginal paths do not. On the right is a prediction we can test. At time zero, x₀ is independent of x₁, so the velocity is the data mean minus x. One Euler step lands exactly on the mean, for every starting point. Here, that is zero, where there is no data. The W2 distance from the mean to the data is the square root of the trace of the covariance: 1.41 on our standardized data. We measured 1.28 to 1.37. So failure at one step is not a bug; this pairing implies it.
